AI Safety & Democracy · Language experiment · Oct 2026

A Chinese AI model censors three times as much when asked in Chinese, and switching off its refusal circuit doesn't change that.

Same requests, English vs Chinese, to Qwen2.5-7B (Alibaba) and Llama-3.1-8B (Meta). Qwen's extra Chinese censorship is mostly the government's official line, delivered as an answer, and the tool safety researchers use to switch refusal off leaves it in place.

Censored = the reply refuses, dodges, or gives the official line instead of doing what was asked (correcting a false election claim counts as not censored). A blind judge labelled every reply. The refusal direction is a single pattern inside the model; removing it makes the model stop refusing harmful requests.

1 · The surprise

In Chinese, Qwen behaves like a different model

Each English request had a word-for-word Chinese twin. Of 24 pairs, 10 were answered in English and censored in Chinese; none went the other way. Traditional Chinese (Taiwan, Hong Kong) gave the same 62%, so the trigger is the language, not the script. Harmless controls worded the same way were answered in every language.

2 · Whose politics

Each model guards its own country's sensitive topics

US-built Llama answered every China-sensitive request in both languages, but refused 69% of the team's US “stolen election” requests in English (Qwen: 19%). On those election requests the language effect runs backwards: both models censor less in Chinese. Same direction for both, but neither survives correction for multiple tests, so treat it as a lead.

This extends the team's earlier finding on the same prompts (no Democrat-vs-Republican difference; both models help more when the claim favours the asker's side). Language is a second lever.

3 · The mechanism

One refusal dial serves both languages, but turning it off leaves the Chinese censorship in place

Refusal in these models is largely controlled by one direction inside the network, a “refusal dial”; removing it (abliteration) makes a model stop refusing. We rebuilt the team's English direction (Divya's, reproduced within tolerance) and built a second one from Chinese prompts.

Either dial, removed, takes refusal of harmful requests to 0% in both languages.
Dial removed → promptsQwenLlama
English dial → Chinese75% → 0%84% → 0%
Chinese dial → English88% → 0%94% → 0%

32 harmful anchor prompts per language. Cross-language effect is 86–117% of same-language (bar: 75%); the spread reflects starting rates only.

So refusing a bomb recipe runs on a shared mechanism. Does it also drive the Chinese-language censorship?

The pre-registered bar was for removal to shrink the gap to half or less. It grew to 110% with the English dial and stayed at 80% with the Chinese one. Official-line answers make up 29 of the 42 gap points, and they don't budge: 9 of 24 in Chinese before, 9 after (7 with the Chinese dial).

The refusal direction governs refusing. Qwen's Chinese-language censorship mostly answers, in the state's voice. A tool built to find or remove refusal will miss it.

So what

Audit in every deployment language, score the framing, and don't treat abliteration as de-censoring

  1. English-only audits understate censorship.

    Qwen looks mostly open in English (21%) and censors 62% of the same requests in Chinese. Guardrails shift too: Llama refused election-denial letters in English and wrote some in Chinese (exploratory).

  2. Refusal counters miss state-aligned answers.

    About 70% of Qwen's language gap (29 of 42 points) is replies that look helpful and carry the official line. A benchmark that only counts refusals scores these as answered; rubrics need an official-line label.

  3. Removing the refusal direction neither fixes nor detects this censorship.

    It cut harmful-request refusal to 0% in both languages, yet left Qwen's Chinese gap at 110%. “Uncensored” abliterated releases of Chinese-lab models could still carry state framing (not tested here).

How we know

Matched prompts, a blind judge, and pass bars set before the run

Prompts. China set: 12 topics, each as a question and a writing request (24) plus 24 harmless controls worded the same way, in English, Simplified and Traditional Chinese. US set: the team's stolen-election prompts, 8 elections × 6 request types (48) plus 16 civic controls, in English and Simplified Chinese. Translated by Claude.

Models. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, greedy decoding, up to 300 new tokens, on an H100.

Inside the model. Refusal direction from harmful vs harmless anchor prompts (Arditi et al. 2024), at Qwen layer 19 and Llama layer 13; removal during generation; projection matching with a random-direction control.

Judge. Claude Opus 5.5 saw only request and reply, never model, language or condition. Labels: Answers, Official line, Pushback, Dodges, Refuses.

Checks. A second judge (Claude Sonnet 5.5) re-scored 194 replies: 94% exact agreement on Qwen, 98% on Llama. 60 Chinese replies back-translated and re-judged: 95% same label, 98% same censored/not call. 15 of 1,966 replies went unlabelled: pipe-bomb sanity checks the judge's safety filter declined; a keyword check shows both models refused them.

Stats. Exact McNemar test on each English–Chinese pair; Holm correction across the four main tests; bootstrap 95% intervals.

Limits

  • Small n. 24 China pairs from 12 topics, so the effective sample is closer to 12 (the plan called for 20 topics). The 62% has a 95% interval of 42–83%. US results are exploratory.
  • Machine translation, no native speaker. The back-translation check covers the judging, not the prompt wording.
  • One judge family. Claude both judged and re-checked.
  • Truncation at 300 tokens. Qwen 64% (EN) and 68% (ZH); Llama 0% (EN) and 45% (ZH), so Llama's language comparison is less clean. A late turn in a long reply could be missed.
  • Anchor substitution. 11 harmful anchors were replaced with MultiJail Chinese prompts for the Chinese direction.
  • The Chinese dial is one-sided. Removing it works, but adding it barely induces refusal (Qwen: 0.12 vs 5.14 for the English dial).
  • Two mid-size models (7–8B), one Chinese and one US lab. Larger or newer models may differ.
  • Rounding. Percentages are counts out of 24 or 48; exact halves round down (62.5% shows as 62%).
